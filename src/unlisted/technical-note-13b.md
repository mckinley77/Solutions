Ask any Course Manager whether winters have got harder and the answer comes back immediately: yes, and the course needs better drainage.

Ask around the clubhouse and you get something different. Next year will be better, it was just another wet winter. It wasn’t, and the records explain why.

## What the records show

Going back through the Met Office rainfall series for Northern Ireland and Scotland, which run from 1836. Six things stand out.

1. **Winters are wetter.** Set the three standard 30-year climate periods against each other and autumn and winter rainfall is up 7% in Northern Ireland and 16% in Scotland. Measured as a trend across the full record since 1932 the rise is larger still, about 14% here and 27% in Scotland. Across the UK the October to March half-year is now 13% wetter than it was in 1961–1990.
2. **The wettest seasons are bunching up.** Five of the ten wettest September-to-February seasons in Northern Ireland since 1836 have come since 2012.
3. **Last winter was exceptional.** September 2025 to February 2026 delivered 863mm across Northern Ireland, the second wettest in 190 years and beaten only by 1877, though in Scotland the same season was unremarkable. Recent storms Austen and Boelo then closed courses that had been parched three months earlier.
4. **Each storm carries more rain.** Rainfall in the 2023/24 storms was about 20% heavier than it would have been without climate change, and rainfall at that level on a storm day is now roughly ten times more likely.
5. **But there are no more storms, and no more wind.** The wind record shows no trend. Naming storms since 2015 has made them memorable, not more frequent.
6. **The rain arrives in sharper bursts.** Warmer air holds more moisture, about 7% more for every degree, and it tends to fall in heavier bursts with fewer days of light rain between. The same annual total, delivered in fewer and sharper events, is a harder drainage challenge.

***The projections are for even wetter winters and more intense downpours in the years ahead.***

Summers are a different question. Northern Ireland’s summer rainfall has not fallen, and dry months are no more frequent than they were. But warmer air dries the ground faster, so drought is felt more keenly than the totals suggest: July 2026 delivered just 27mm, the eighth driest July since 1836.

<p class="pull-quote">Courses need to be ready for both extremes.</p>

## Why it feels worse than the figures

After forty years on golf course drainage, my own view is that the rainfall is only half the story. Existing drains have sealed over. Machinery is heavier and used more intensively on wetter soils. Staff numbers are lower. Tree cover and leaf fall have increased, and the clearing falls at exactly the time drainage work ought to be under way.

<p class="pull-quote">Enough rain to overwhelm a drainage system in an afternoon.</p>

The same winter a club coped with in 1990 now does more damage, takes longer to recover from and costs more to put right. Through 2023 and the winter that followed, closures of 60 days or more were common across Northern Ireland, and some courses in Ireland lost more than 100 days.

## Two clubs that coped well

Dunmurry Golf Club is built on heavy clay and ought to have been among the worst affected. Years of sanding and vertidraining the fairways kept closures to 32 days, and the club has invested in targeted drainage in the problem areas.

At Killymoon Golf Club, a switch to lightweight robotic mowers has reduced machinery damage and improved turf quality and permeability. Cutting fairways when traditional machines cannot.

Neither did anything complicated. Both did something consistently, over several years and more, which is the part most clubs find hardest.

## Keep your own records

Peter Smith at Bathgate has logged monthly rainfall at his course since 2013. When he tells his committee this was the wettest winter in eleven years, he can prove it, and that survives a change of Convenor in a way that opinions do not.

A number of Course Managers in Northern Ireland keep similar records, but none of it is collated or published. National figures tell you what fell across the country. They don’t tell you what fell on your own low-lying ninth.

**If you keep rainfall or course closure records at your course, I would be glad to hear from you.** Contributors would get their own club’s comparison back, and anything published would be in aggregate unless a club wanted otherwise.

## In short

Winters are wetter, the wettest seasons are coming closer together, and the rain falls harder when it comes. At the same time, existing drainage on many courses will not have the capacity to absorb it. That combination, rather than the rainfall alone, is why winters are harder to manage than they were thirty years ago.

The instinct in that first answer is right: most clubs will need better drainage sooner or later. But there are three things a Course Manager can do now.

Take this evidence to the Committee while the ground is still wet, so the case rests on a proven trend, rather than on one bad winter.

Do the consistent, inexpensive work that Dunmurry and Killymoon did, which costs a fraction of a drainage scheme and gets more out of the drainage a club already has.

And keep your own rainfall and closure records, so that next time you are arguing with your own local evidence. These three approaches can make the case for investment winnable.

<p class="pull-quote">Projections point to more downpours continuing.</p>
