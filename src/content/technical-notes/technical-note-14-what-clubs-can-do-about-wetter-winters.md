**Most of the solutions cost very little or nothing. Just simple changes to course management.**

[Technical Note 13](/technical-notes/technical-note-13-golf-in-a-changing-climate/) set out the evidence that autumn and winter rainfall in Northern Ireland and Scotland has risen over the last ninety years, the wettest seasons are bunching up, and each storm now carries appreciably more rain than the same storm would have carried forty years ago. That note covered only the evidence but this one deals with what a club can actually do about it, drawn from my earlier technical notes.

It arrives at an awkward moment. We have had weeks of dry weather and clubs may still be cutting or clearing leaves or starting winter construction projects.

That is the recurring problem at this time of year. Almost everything worth doing is best done while the ground is dry and firm. By the time a committee becomes concerned, maybe in January, with the course shut and the members unhappy, the window has closed for another year. The most useful measures in this note are either impossible or actively damaging on saturated ground.

**The best winter surfaces are not created in winter.**

## Key points for golf clubs

- Most courses already contain more drainage than anyone realises, and much of it has stopped working
- Restoring existing drains is cheaper, quicker and far less disruptive than installing new ones
- Managing course traffic costs almost nothing and often achieves more than new pipework
- Short grass is firmer and drier than long grass, and wide grass routes beat narrow worn ones
- Vertidraining, sanding and aeration work much better in dry conditions and cause damage in wet ones
- Lighter machinery permits cutting on more days on wet fairways, protects drainage you have already paid for and releases staff time
- Create proper written records and drawings. A club that knows what is in the ground stops paying for the same mistake over and over again.

## 1. Before spending anything, find out what you already have

Ask most clubs where their drains run and you will get a shrug. Pipes were laid over decades, often without records, and whoever knew has long since left the committee. Yet in my experience most winter problems are not caused by a shortage of drainage. They are caused by water failing to reach drainage that is already there.

Finding out is neither difficult nor expensive:

- **Probing.** A steel rod will find gravel trenches surprisingly quickly once you have the knack.
- **Trial pits.** A small excavation shows the pipe, its depth, the backfill, and whether the gravel is clean or sealed.
- **The water test.** Scrape the turf off a gravel drain and pour water in. If it disappears, that length is alive. Work down the line and keep testing until you reach the outfall.
- **Dye testing.** Environmentally friendly dye traces direction and connection. Use it sparingly. It can take a long time to reach an outfall and you do not want complaints of pollution downstream.
- **Drone survey.** In a warm dry spell old drain runs often show through the turf. A dry frosty winter day can sometimes be as revealing as a dry summer.
- **Manholes and level surveys.** Open any chambers you have. Where gradients are in doubt, get levels taken, because drains laid with inadequate fall will silt up and fail.

**Then record and write down what you find.** A basic course asset map showing drainage runs, outfalls, irrigation lines, tree positions and species is the cheapest and highest-return piece of work a club can do, and the one most likely to be skipped, because it shows no immediate result on the course. It's what stops a contractor digging up a working pipe, or severing an irrigation main nobody knew was there. Kept as a living document and handed to each new committee, it pays for itself in avoided mistakes alone.

## 2. Check the outfalls

This is the cheapest job on the list and the most frequently overlooked. A perfectly good drainage system will not shift a drop if the water cannot get out at the end.

Walk the ditches and streams. Look for sediment build-up, vegetation growth and debris. Check whether outlets are submerged during wet periods. Outfalls should be visible, clear, and discharging freely after rainfall. Half an hour with a spade and strimmer can restore a system that everyone had written off.

## 3. Refresh the drains you already have

Most golf course drainage relies on gravel-filled trenches to get water down to the pipe. Over the years that gravel seals with organic matter and fine soil, grasses grow over, and it stops accepting water. The pipe below may still be working perfectly while the drain above it has effectively been made useless.

Restoring it is often straightforward. Remove the sealed surface layer with a narrow-bladed turf cutter, a Wizz Wheel, or a trencher set to shallow depth, and top up with clean gravel:

- coarse gravel, around 20mm, for topping drains in the rough
- fine natural gravel, around 6mm, in fairways
- **not sand**, which seals too quickly and often washes out in heavy rain

Equally, do not seed or turf over a drain to tidy it up. It will block almost immediately. There are high-profile areas where appearance is important, but where drainage performance is the priority, leave the gravel flush and open to the surface.

**A drain that has been refreshed costs a fraction of a drain that has been replaced and creates much less course damage.**

## 4. Manage the traffic: the cheapest improvement on any course

Much of the damage seen through a wet winter is not caused by a lack of drainage at all. It is caused by traffic on saturated ground: rutting, smearing, loss of grass cover and, worst of all, surface sealing that stops water reaching the drains and makes the original problem worse.

The damage is predictable. It happens in the same places every year: walk-offs from greens, path entrances and exits, around tees, pinch points, shaded corners. Because it is predictable, it can be planned for.

- **Vary the routes.** Slightly worn turf recovers quickly; badly worn turf takes months. Move routes before the damage appears, not after.
- **Widen the pinch points.** Taking an entrance from three yards to six halves the concentration of traffic across it. That one change is free and often transforms a problem area.
- **Use lighter machinery, or none.** A backpack blower in place of a tractor-mounted one. Fewer turning movements on soft ground. Lightweight robots can transform wet fairways. More often than not the right decision is simply to delay the operation until conditions improve.
- **Protect the vulnerable areas.** Temporary routes varied regularly, matting moved regularly, restricted access where it matters. Not always popular, but far better than a scarred surround that takes until July to recover.

## 5. Keep the grass as short as possible

With preferred lies in operation through most of the winter there is little advantage in long rough, and several disadvantages. Short, regularly cut turf dries faster, is firmer underfoot, resists wear better and suffers less machinery damage. Balls are easier to find, which no member has ever complained about.

## 6. Cut wide grass paths

Hard paths are expensive, and they often simply move the problem to a muddy, compacted area at each end. Before building more of them, ask whether the wear can be spread instead.

A broad, regularly cut grass route is cheaper, more attractive and perfectly adequate in most locations. Firm grass is also more pleasant to walk on than tarmac. Make these routes as wide as you can, and in wet areas there is no reason not to go to full fairway width. Link greens, tees and the main lines of play so that golfers can walk the course on short grass from the first hole to the last. Paved paths will still be needed in the very heaviest traffic, but far fewer of them.

The timing matters more than any of this. The firmness you want in January begins the previous May. Cutting should **start early in the growing season and be maintained**. It is not something that can be done at any time in response to a wet spell.

The same thinking applies to the carry from the tee. Many clubs have gradually extended the rough between tee and fairway, which punishes the beginner and the ageing golfer and creates an unpleasant winter walk. A narrow mown strip through it becomes compacted and muddy. Taper the fairway back towards the tee instead or cut wide grass paths. It is the same mowing, simply distributed differently. It is too late to create new routes in autumn or winter, as fresh ones would cut up badly. But watch where the wear shows over the coming months, and plan generous routes to cut from early next spring, so they are firm and mature by the following winter.

## 7. Prepare the winter measures in early autumn

**Temporary greens.** Nobody likes them, but they keep a course open through frost, flooding and maintenance, and they take traffic off the main greens when those greens are at their most vulnerable. The key is preparing them before they are needed. Mark out suitable areas in September, repair divots with sand and seed, give them a few cuts and a topdressing, and make them large enough to move the hole as wear develops. Choose positions that still make the hole interesting to play, and vary those positions each year so the same spot is not compacted every winter. A few hundred pounds here can save course closures, and with it the green fees, bar and shop income that each closure costs.

**Winter tees.** Large tees in good condition may last all year, though rarely on par threes where divot damage is concentrated. Where space allows, a permanent winter tee well away from the main playing area can work. A simpler answer is to use a good-quality range mat on the normal tee, moved at least weekly to spread the wear. My own club has used that approach for years and avoided permanent winter tees on almost every hole.

## 8. Vertidraining, slitting and the rest: what each one actually does

These operations get used interchangeably, but they are not interchangeable. Each does a different job, and each has conditions in which it does nothing at all.

- **Deep tine aeration.** The deepest of them, and usually called vertidraining. Machines such as the Vertidrain drive solid or hollow tines well below the compacted layer and heave the soil, fracturing it and opening the movement of water, air and roots. It is the one that produces genuinely deep rooting, which is why it pays back twice: better winter infiltration, and turf that copes far better with next summer's drought. On its own, it will not solve drainage problems in clay soils that are inherently impermeable, but on soils with some natural permeability it can reduce the need for extensive pipework.
- **Slitting.** Shallower and much quicker, using narrow blades to open the surface. It gives temporary relief and lets water in during a wet spell, and it can be repeated through the season. What it does not do is reach the compacted layer, so it treats the symptom rather than the cause. Useful as a maintenance operation, not as a solution.
- **Linear decompaction.** Machines such as the Shockwave cut deep vertical slits that fracture compacted layers across large areas quickly. Effective where drains are already in place and working, where compaction is the limiting factor, and where the soil has some permeability. It works at a shallower depth than deep tine aeration, so it does less for root development, and like all aeration the effect reduces as the soil consolidates again, so it needs repeating.
- **Gravel banding.** The one most often reached for first, because it is quick, cheap and easily hired in. It is not a drainage system but a maintenance tool for getting surface water to existing drains with minimal disturbance. It works over short distances, twenty metres at most, and only where it feeds into drains that are themselves working, or into permeable soil. The grooves seal at the surface and need repeating, though that is cheap enough to do. Used where there are working drains to feed, it is effective and worth doing. Used where the drains below are blocked or missing, it is money wasted.

One rule covers all four of the operations above. **None of them replaces drainage.** They help water reach drains that work. Where the drains do not work, or there are none, all you have achieved is to move water through the soil more easily to somewhere it still cannot go, often creating saturation.

And all four depend on timing. Late summer and early autumn, in firm dry ground, is when this work is worth doing. Heavy machines on soft ground cause more damage than the operation is worth, and compaction inflicted that way can take years to recover.

## 9. Take weight off the course

Good drainage is only half the answer. Managing machinery traffic when soils are saturated is just as important, and it is the half most clubs have not addressed.

A conventional fairway mower, with fuel and operator, can weigh up to two tonnes. On saturated ground that leaves a greenkeeper with two poor options: mow and damage the surface, or stop mowing and let the grass grow long until conditions improve. A commercial robotic mower weighs around 100kg and changes that calculation entirely.

The usual argument for robots is labour and fuel. In my view the bigger prize is simpler: more days on which mowing is possible, and therefore more days on which the course plays well. Frequent light cutting also produces denser, finer, drier turf, and the reduction in traffic protects the drainage investment a club has already made. Staff hours released go into work that lasts: maintaining drains, blowing leaves with a backpack rather than a tractor-mounted unit on soft ground, bunkers, woodland, presentation.

For most clubs the sensible route in the short term is a hybrid one: robots on the wettest and most vulnerable fairways, conventional machines retained for drier areas, striping, events and periods of rapid growth. ## 10. Look hard at the trees

Tree management is one of the largest unnoticed charges on a maintenance budget. The cost never appears as a single line; it is spread thin across the year in leaf clearing, branch cutting, root damage and slower inefficient cutting, which is exactly why clubs lose track of it.

On wet clay parkland the damage goes further than cost. Mowers forced into repeated tight turns around closely spaced trunks churn and rut the turf around them, and rutted, compacted ground drains worse than the turf beside it. A planting problem becomes a drainage problem.

- **Commission a tree survey.** What is there, what was planted when, and what the long-term plan for spacing and thinning actually is. Without one, each committee adds a little more and nobody sees the cumulative effect until it is mature and expensive to reverse.
- **Mark trees while they are still in leaf.** Too many healthy young trees have been felled because a bare winter silhouette was mistaken for a dead one. Make the decisions in early autumn, when leaf cover shows plainly which trees are genuinely struggling.
- **Watch the species near drains.** Willow and poplar are the worst offenders, but almost any tree will find a crack in a pipe eventually. Where a drain has to pass close to trees, use solid unperforated pipe for that length, a small extra cost at installation against a much larger repair later.
- **Think about leaf burden when planting.** Sycamore drops enormous quantities of leaf onto playing surfaces; beech litters tees and greens with hard seed pods. Keep them away from the main playing areas. A mixed planting of native species suited to our climate is always the safer long-term bet.

Standing deadwood in an out-of-the-way corner is valuable habitat and worth keeping. Close to a green or a fairway, safety and playability come first.

## Final thoughts

Not one of the measures in this note is complicated, and most of them cost very little or nothing at all. They are about managing turf, traffic and machinery more carefully, and about finding out what a club already has before it invests in more.

The expensive mistake is to do it the other way round: a major drainage scheme installed across a course nobody has investigated, over existing drains that still had decades of life in them, into soil nobody has de-compacted, beneath trees nobody has surveyed, and mown by a two-tonne machine that cannot go out until April.

Most clubs will need to invest in drainage sooner or later. The clubs that get real value from it are the ones that do these tried and tested things in a regular and timely way.

*Simple solutions are often the best.*
