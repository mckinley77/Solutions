September 2025 to February 2026 was the second wettest autumn and winter in Northern Ireland since records began in 1836 and, in October 2026, Storms Austen and Boelo closed courses that had been parched three months earlier.

The usual response around golf clubs is that it was just another wet winter and next year will be better. It wasn’t, and the records will explain why. The rainfall data, the projections and forty years of working on golf course drainage all point the same way: the problem is no longer just how much rain falls, but the sheer intensity of it, and how we prepare our courses for that within limited budgets and staff numbers.

This article sets out the evidence: what has actually changed in our rainfall and storms, and by how much. What clubs can do about it will be the subject of the next article.

## What the evidence shows

- **UK winters are wetter.** The October to March half-year is 13% wetter than it was in 1961–1990 [[1](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/about-us/news-and-media/media-centre/weather-and-climate-news/2026/climate-extremes-are-becoming-the-new-normal-for-the-uk)].
- Northern Ireland has seen it too. Autumn and winter rainfall here has risen about 14% since 1932, and the seasons since 2021 average 10% above the 1961–1990 figure [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)].
- The extremes have risen with it. Five of the ten wettest autumn and winter seasons since 1836 have come since 2012, and September 2025 to February 2026 was the second wettest in the whole record [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)].
- **Storm rainfall is around 20% heavier** than it would have been without climate change, and rainfall at that level on individual storm days has become roughly ten times more likely. Seasons as wet as 2023/24 are at least four times more likely [[30](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/autumn-and-winter-storms-over-uk-and-ireland-are-becoming-wetter-due-to-climate-change)].
- **Storms are not more frequent, and not windier**. The wind record shows no trend [[25](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2024/why-have-there-been-so-many-storms-in-the-uk-this-year)].
- **Rain increasingly arrives in sharp bursts rather than steady falls,** which is what overwhelms drainage [[36](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2019.0542), [38](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.14337)].
- **Projections point to wetter winters and more intense downpours continuing** [[9](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/approach/collaboration/ukcp/summaries-headline-findings)].

As a result of these changes and future projections, courses need highly efficient drainage to cope with rain arriving in heavier bursts, on ground that is wet for longer.

## Are winters getting wetter, and will it continue?

Yes, and in Northern Ireland as well as across the UK. The October to March half-year is now 13% wetter across the UK than it was in 1961–1990 [[1](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/about-us/news-and-media/media-centre/weather-and-climate-news/2026/climate-extremes-are-becoming-the-new-normal-for-the-uk)]. In Northern Ireland autumn and winter rainfall has risen about 14% since 1932 and about 22% across the full record since 1837, with the seasons since 2021 running close to 10% above the 1961–1990 average [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)].

The projections point to wetter winters still, and to heavier downpours when the rain comes [[9](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/approach/collaboration/ukcp/summaries-headline-findings)].

### Northern Ireland figures

Northern Ireland had 1,375mm of rain in 2023, the highest in 21 years, the previous higher total being 1,411mm in 2002. Only the all-time record of 1,418mm, set in 1872, was higher [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)].

So 2002 and 2023 are the second and third wettest years in a series going back to 1836, and 2023 was 19% above the 1991–2020 average [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)].

### The trends across the UK and Ireland

- **UK:** the winter half-year (October to March) in the most recent decade, 2016–2025, was 3% wetter than 1991–2020 and 13% wetter than 1961–1990, while summer rainfall changed little [[1](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/about-us/news-and-media/media-centre/weather-and-climate-news/2026/climate-extremes-are-becoming-the-new-normal-for-the-uk), [2](https://www.rmets.org/news/state-uk-climate-2025-report-climate-extremes-are-becoming-new-normal-uk)].
- **England and Wales:** October 2023 to March 2024 was the wettest winter half-year on record, and six of the ten wettest winter half-years in a series going back to 1767 have happened since 2000 [[3](https://www.preventionweb.net/publication/documents-and-publications/state-uk-climate-2025)].
- **Scotland:** the increase has been larger still. On the same Met Office series, Scottish autumn and winter rainfall has risen about 27% since 1932, roughly twice Northern Ireland’s 14%, and from a much wetter base: 857mm for the 1961–1990 period against 623mm here. Four of Scotland’s ten wettest autumn and winter seasons since 1836 have also fallen since 2012 [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)]. The one exception is the season just past, which was unremarkable in Scotland: 941mm, ranked only fortieth [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)].
- **Northern Ireland**: autumn and winter rainfall has always been variable, but the direction is upward. The fitted trend rises about 22% across the full Met Office record from 1837, and about 14% since 1932. Against the standard 1961–1990 baseline the decade 2016–2025 was 3.9% wetter and the seasons since 2021 are 9.8% wetter, while annual totals are up about 11% since 1932 [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)].
- **What has changed is the return of extremes**. Five of the ten wettest September-to-February seasons since 1836 have occurred since 2012, and three of them since 2016. The season just past, September 2025 to February 2026, delivered 863mm, the second wettest in the entire record and beaten only by 1877 [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)]. 2023 was Northern Ireland’s third wettest calendar year since records began in 1836 [[4](https://irishnews.com/news/northern-ireland/figures-show-steady-rainfall-increase-for-northern-ireland-over-recent-years-6BYFO4KKIBBLZDLLRBT56OKBZQ)].

Over the last twenty-five years the direction is clear: autumn and winter rainfall averaged 637mm in the 2000s, 667mm in the 2010s and 684mm in the seasons since 2021, the highest of any comparable period in the record [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)].

- **Republic of Ireland:** rainfall rose by 7% between 1961–1990 and 1991–2020, and the largest increases were in the west and north [[6](https://teagasc.ie/news--events/daily/weather-trends-over-the-past-60-years/)].
- **Flooding:** UK river records show a strong, consistent national rise in high flows, which matches the shift towards wetter winters [[1](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/about-us/news-and-media/media-centre/weather-and-climate-news/2026/climate-extremes-are-becoming-the-new-normal-for-the-uk)].

### Projections

**The UK Climate Projections 2018 (UKCP18)** project a significant increase in winter rainfall and a significant decrease in summer rainfall, with more intense storms when summer rain does fall [[9](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/approach/collaboration/ukcp/summaries-headline-findings)].

For drainage design, the intensity of rain matters as much as the totals:

- Each degree of warming lets the atmosphere hold about 7% more moisture, so heavy rainfall events are expected to become more intense [[8](https://www.thestory.ie/2023/08/18/ireland-will-have-to-prepare-for-much-heavier-rainfall-storm-surges-and-coastal-flooding-because-of-climate-change-says-met-eireann-advisory)].
- Newer high-resolution UKCP18 modelling shows a marked shift towards more intense hourly rain and less light rain [[10](https://www.ukclimaterisk.org/?p=2126)].

### Caveats

- Individual years will still vary a lot. Some cold, dry winters will still happen. For example, winter 2024/25 had below-average rainfall, especially in Northern Ireland [[11](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2025/winter-and-february-2025-weather-review)].
- Summers are more complicated. Northern Ireland’s summer rainfall totals have not fallen, and dry months are no more frequent than they were. But individual dry spells have been severe: July 2026 delivered just 27mm, the eighth driest July since 1836 [[39](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/uk-and-regional-series)].
- Two things explain why drought is felt more keenly while the statistics barely move. Warmer air draws more moisture from the ground, so the same rainfall supports less soil moisture. And monthly totals cannot show the dry spells that open up when rain arrives in fewer, heavier bursts. Drier summers are also projected [[8](https://www.thestory.ie/2023/08/18/ireland-will-have-to-prepare-for-much-heavier-rainfall-storm-surges-and-coastal-flooding-because-of-climate-change-says-met-eireann-advisory)]. For golf clubs the implication is unchanged: be ready for both extremes.

## How climate change is affecting golf: published articles

Most of the coverage falls into two groups: the 2018 R&A report that first set out the case, and reporting from clubs and greenkeepers after the very wet winter of 2023/24.

### Climate Coalition "Game Changer" report (2018)

The most widely cited source, supported by the R&A [[12](https://www.golfcoursearchitecture.net/content/uk-courses-suffering-from-climate-change-says-new-report), [13](https://climate.leeds.ac.uk/news/climate-change-driving-golf-off-the-green/), [14](https://todays-golfer.com/news-and-events/general-news/2018/february/links-courses-at-risk-of-disappearing-by-end-of-the-century-says-climate-coalition-report), [34](https://www.preventionweb.net/publication/game-changer-how-climate-change-impacting-sports-uk)].

- Warned of more unplayable holes, more winter course closures and disruption to professional tournaments because of increased rainfall [[12](https://www.golfcoursearchitecture.net/content/uk-courses-suffering-from-climate-change-says-new-report), [34](https://www.preventionweb.net/publication/game-changer-how-climate-change-impacting-sports-uk)].
- Said wetter, warmer autumns and winters damage grass and greens and increase disease [[34](https://www.preventionweb.net/publication/game-changer-how-climate-change-impacting-sports-uk)].
- Found the Greater Glasgow area lost 20% of its playing time in 2016/17 compared with 2006/07 [[34](https://www.preventionweb.net/publication/game-changer-how-climate-change-impacting-sports-uk)].
- The R&A's Steve Isaac said he thought golf was more affected by climate change than any sport except skiing [[13](https://climate.leeds.ac.uk/news/climate-change-driving-golf-off-the-green/), [34](https://www.preventionweb.net/publication/game-changer-how-climate-change-impacting-sports-uk)].
- STRI research quoted in the report linked wetter autumns and winters to softer surfaces, green closures and muddy conditions [[12](https://www.golfcoursearchitecture.net/content/uk-courses-suffering-from-climate-change-says-new-report)].

### The 2023/24 wet winter

- **The Golf Business:** a Club Management Association of Ireland survey found clubs closed on two to four times as many days as in previous winters. Some links courses flooded for the first time [[15](https://thegolfbusiness.co.uk/2024/04/clubs-closed-more-for-than-twice-as-normal-due-to-winter-weather/)].
- **The Golf Business:** wet weather continued into spring and slowed recovery. Dromoland Castle closed for 29 days between 1 January and spring [[16](https://thegolfbusiness.co.uk/?p=928801)].
- **NCG / BIGGA:** a greenkeeper survey. Bathgate GC's course manager has kept monthly rainfall records since 2013 and has seen a steady rise, peaking in winter 2023/24. Explains in plain language how a high water table stops maintenance and how warmer, wetter conditions increase turf disease. Useful to show committees [[17](https://www.nationalclubgolfer.com/club/features/greenkeeper-flooded-golf-courses-effects/)].
- **NCG – Golf in the rain:** one club manager reported losing about a quarter of days to rain from November to January [[18](https://www.nationalclubgolfer.com/club/features/golf-in-the-rain/)].
- **Northern Golfer – Winter woes:** a greenkeeper argues for long-term planning, with drainage projects for the wettest areas and better traffic management [[19](https://www.northerngolfer.co.uk/winter-woes/)].
- **BIGGA – Storms, droughts and mild winters:** course managers describe swinging between waterlogging and drought within a few years [[20](https://www.bigga.org.uk/news-listing/storms-droughts-and-mild-winters.html)].

**My own findings in Northern Ireland:** these reports match what I have seen in my work here with local clubs. Over forty years the bigger change has not been the overall rainfall, but the intensity and frequency in winter months.

Machinery is heavier yet more widely used on wetter soils, staff numbers are lower, tree cover and leaf fall have increased, and the rain arrives in heavier bursts. The same winter a club could cope with in 1990 now does more damage, takes longer to recover from and costs more to put right. I’ve seen few courses that have responded effectively to these new conditions but many are now recognising the challenge ahead.

Across Northern Ireland through the very wet year of 2023 and the winter that followed, closures of sixty days or more were common, and in Ireland as a whole some courses lost more than a hundred days in 2023 [[40](https://www.irishtimes.com/sport/2026/02/18/its-just-a-bad-spell-irelands-sodden-golf-courses-hope-to-bounce-back/)].

One shining example was Dunmurry Golf Club, built on heavy clay soils. Years of sanding and vertidraining their fairways kept closures down to 32 days, and the club has wisely invested in the few problem areas that needed additional drainage. Another approach at Killymoon Golf Club has been their major switch to lightweight robotic mowers that have reduced machinery damage and improved turf quality and permeability. These and other proposals to deal with the problems outlined here will be discussed in Technical Note 14 to follow this article.

### Older background

An early STRI study of 30 Scottish courses was started after rain washed out large parts of the season in summer 2002, the same wet year that appears in the Northern Ireland data [[24](https://www.golfcourseindustry.com/news/gloomy-forecast-threatens-to-drive-golf-industry-off-course)].

My own findings: across those two decades drainage has moved from a routine maintenance item to a central part of course planning for the clubs I work with.

**Gap:** no published coverage specific to Northern Ireland golf was found. The Irish reporting is mostly about the Republic, and the UK reporting focuses on Scotland and England. This article sets out to fill that gap.

## Are we getting more storms, or stronger winds?

Not so far, at least not in any way the records can detect. Projections point to a modest increase in winter storms over the British Isles later this century, but a clearer increase in storms that bring extreme wind and extreme rain together.

### What the records show

- The Met Office says that, although the UK climate as a whole is getting wetter, there are no compelling trends in increasing storminess in recent decades [[25](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2024/why-have-there-been-so-many-storms-in-the-uk-this-year)].
- Wind data since the mid-20th century show no clear trend in daily mean wind speed over the UK. Wind varies so much from day to day and year to year that any climate signal is hard to pick out [[26](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/binaries/content/assets/metofficegovuk/pdf/research/ukcp/ukcp18-factsheet-storms.pdf)].
- If anything, wind speeds have fallen. Earlier State of the UK Climate reports found a downward trend in UK annual mean wind speed from 1969 to 2024, consistent with the global picture [[35](https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.70010)].
- Storms only started being named in 2015. That period is far too short to show a trend [[25](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2024/why-have-there-been-so-many-storms-in-the-uk-this-year)].

### What the projections say

- UKCP18 projects that winter wind speeds will increase in the second half of the 21st century, along with more frequent winter storms [[27](https://acct.metoffice.gov.uk/research/news/2021/recent-trends-and-future-projections-of-uk-storm-activity)].
- Storm tracks are expected to move. More winter storms, including disproportionately more severe ones, are projected to cross the UK, with fewer passing over Iceland and southern Europe [[27](https://acct.metoffice.gov.uk/research/news/2021/recent-trends-and-future-projections-of-uk-storm-activity)].
- Newcastle University and Met Office research found climate change will increase extreme winter storms that combine strong winds with heavy rainfall over the UK and Ireland. One reason is that a stronger jet stream, shifted further south, steers storms across warmer, moister air [[28](https://www.agriland.ie/farming-news/research-ireland-and-uk-face-increase-in-extreme-winter-storms/)].

### Why storms seem more frequent

- Naming makes them memorable. Babet, Ciarán and Éowyn stick in the mind in a way that unnamed storms in the 1980s and 1990s did not.
- Each storm now drops more rain, so a storm of the same strength does more damage on a course. Ground is also more often already saturated when the next storm arrives [[30](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/autumn-and-winter-storms-over-uk-and-ireland-are-becoming-wetter-due-to-climate-change)].
- Some recent seasons have been very busy. Ten storms were named between September 2023 and January 2024 [[25](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2024/why-have-there-been-so-many-storms-in-the-uk-this-year)]. That sits within the large natural variation from one year to the next, and does not amount to an upward trend.
- Storms have always tended to arrive in clusters over the North Atlantic, with quiet spells between busy ones. That is a long-standing feature of our weather rather than a new one, and research into whether climate change will alter it remains inconclusive [[41](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41612-020-00152-9)].

## Wetter storms: the key finding

A study of the 2023/24 storm season by World Weather Attribution, with the Met Office and Met Éireann, provides direct evidence that storms are delivering more rain [[30](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/autumn-and-winter-storms-over-uk-and-ireland-are-becoming-wetter-due-to-climate-change), [31](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/about-us/news-and-media/media-centre/weather-and-climate-news/2024/climate-change-drives-increase-in-storm-rainfall)]:

- **More rain per storm:** rainfall during autumn and winter storms in 2023/24 was made around 20% more intense by human-caused climate change. Rainfall at that level on individual storm days has become about ten times more likely [[30](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/autumn-and-winter-storms-over-uk-and-ireland-are-becoming-wetter-due-to-climate-change), [31](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/about-us/news-and-media/media-centre/weather-and-climate-news/2024/climate-change-drives-increase-in-storm-rainfall)].
- **More rain over the season:** World Weather Attribution puts the increase in October-to-March rainfall at somewhere between 6% and 25%, and finds that seasons as wet as 2023/24 have become at least four times more likely, now expected about once every twenty years[[30](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/autumn-and-winter-storms-over-uk-and-ireland-are-becoming-wetter-due-to-climate-change), [32](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/grantham/publications/all-publications/autumn-and-winter-storms-in-the-uk-2023-24-.php)]
- **No clear change in wind:** the influence of climate change on storm winds was less clear, with average wind on stormy days found to have decreased slightly [[30](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/autumn-and-winter-storms-over-uk-and-ireland-are-becoming-wetter-due-to-climate-change), [33](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-18518.html)].

So storms are no more frequent than they were, but each one now carries more rain, and seasons as wet as 2023/24 have become far more likely [[32](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/grantham/publications/all-publications/autumn-and-winter-storms-in-the-uk-2023-24-.php)].

### Why warmer air means wetter storms

Warm air holds more moisture than cold air, about 7% more for every degree of warming. A storm crossing the Atlantic today therefore carries more water than the same storm would have carried forty years ago, and drops more of it when it arrives.

There is a second effect. As that moisture condenses into rain it releases heat, and that heat gives the storm more energy. So the rain is not simply heavier; the storm itself can be more vigorous. The effect is strongest in short, intense bursts — the sort that overwhelm a drainage system in an afternoon — where rainfall can rise at around twice the 7% rate [[36](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2019.0542)].

It also falls differently. The extra moisture tends to arrive in heavier bursts rather than as steadier rain, with fewer days of light rain in between [[38](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.14337)]. For a drainage system that is the harder pattern: the same annual total delivered in fewer, sharper events.

None of this means more storms, or windier ones. Wind is driven by other things besides warmth, and the projections for it remain modest and uncertain [[37](https://doi.org/10.1038/ngeo2783)]. The rainfall picture is far clearer: when storms come, they bring more water than they used to.

That is the core of the case for drainage investment. A course has to handle more water arriving in each event, and it has to do so when the ground is already saturated from wetter winter seasons.

### Keeping our own records

Peter Smith at Bathgate in West Lothian has logged monthly rainfall at his course since 2013, and when he tells his committee this was the wettest winter in eleven years, he can prove it [[17](https://www.nationalclubgolfer.com/club/features/greenkeeper-flooded-golf-courses-effects/)]. A number of Northern Ireland course managers keep similar records but, as far as I am aware, none of it is collated or published, so it is not available to other clubs that would benefit from it.

That seems a waste. National figures tell you what fell across the province; they don’t tell you what fell on your own low-lying ninth. If you keep rainfall or course closure records, I would be glad to hear from you.

## Conclusion

Three findings matter for anyone planning course work. Rainfall in Northern Ireland has risen: annual totals are up about 11% since 1932 and autumn and winter rainfall by about 14%, with five of the ten wettest autumn and winter seasons since 1836 falling since 2012. The rain that does fall is heavier, with storm rainfall around 20% more intense than it would have been without climate change, and arriving in sharper bursts. And the projections point the same way for the rest of this century.

At the same time, drainage on many courses will not have the capacity to absorb the increased rainfall. Existing drains have sealed over, machinery is heavier and more intensively used, reducing permeability, staff numbers are lower, and there is more leaf litter to clear at exactly the time drainage work should be under way. That combination, rather than the rainfall alone, is why winters are harder to manage than they were thirty years ago.

The next article looks at what clubs can actually do about it, within the budgets and staff they have. A shared regional record of rainfall and closures would be a good place to start.
